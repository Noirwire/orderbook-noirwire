# Security

This program runs a private order book for spot and perpetual markets on a private rollup. It keeps every trader's tokens in custody accounts it alone can pay out of, and every trader's balances, positions and resting orders in accounts that other traders and the public cannot read through the private endpoint. If you find a way to move tokens you did not deposit, to trade or cancel for a seat you do not own, to read a ledger, a book or another trader's view, or to make the custody balance disagree with the sum of the seats, please tell us privately before telling anyone else.

## Reporting a vulnerability

Email **ph1l1ph@proton.me**.

Include what you found, how to reproduce it, and what an attacker gains. A proof of concept helps. Please do not open a public issue, and do not test against seats, markets or custody accounts that are not yours.

You will get an acknowledgement, and we will keep you informed while we work on a fix. We will credit you when the fix ships unless you would rather we did not.

## What matters most

- Anything that pays tokens out of custody other than `withdraw` signed by the seat's owner, to the owner's own token account, within margin, or `collect_fees` signed by the admin, for no more than the fee seat holds of that token, and for collateral only while no market has a recorded shortfall.
- Anything that pays a balance out of a custody account other than the one its token was registered with. The collateral token is fixed when the exchange is created, a token's mint, custody nonce and custody account are fixed when it is registered, and a market's tokens are fixed when it is created. A mint registered at a second index must name the custody nonce it already has, and `transfer_between_balances`, which moves value between the collateral and a spot balance with no token moving, refuses a spot token kept in any custody account other than the collateral's.
- Anything that moves tokens into a custody account without crediting a seat, other than a plain token transfer from outside this program. `collect_fees` refuses every registered custody account as its destination.
- Anything that credits a seat without the same instruction moving the tokens into custody.
- Anything that lets a trader's deposit take a token's custody above the cap the admin set for it, or makes a deposit's outcome tell a depositor who does not hold the amount anything about the seat the deposit is for.
- Anything that makes the insurance seat and the fee seat together pay more than a liquidated target's deficit at the mark, or pay any part of a liquidator's bonus at the prices that liquidation uses, or makes the fee seat pay while the insurance seat still holds collateral. The penalty a liquidator earns is capped by what the target can pay at the mark (`RULES.md` section 8), so a bankrupt target is taken over at the mark, and two seats of one owner cannot turn a price gap into a liquidator's bonus paid out of insurance. The deficit itself is still paid out of insurance, and then out of the fee seat's collateral, as on any perpetuals venue; "What this program does not guarantee" says when that happens and how large it can get.
- Anything that lets a key other than one of a view's four order keys place, cancel or liquidate for that seat, or lets an order key withdraw, close or replace keys.
- Anything that lets a reader who is not the owner read a view, or anyone at all read the ledger or a book, through the private endpoint.
- Anything that moves a market's mark other than `sync_price` reading the Pyth account the market is bound to: within the move limit, or by settling a dislocation a second observation confirmed (`RULES.md` section 9). On a test network's build only, a market bound to no source is also priced by the oracle key within the move limit and by the admin's reset. The admin's `set_price_source` clears a paused market's mark to none, with no position and no order on it, and the next `sync_price` sets its first mark.
- Anything that opens a market on a first mark nothing bounded: a market's first `sync_price`, and on a test network's unbound market the admin's reset, which is the only way such a feed gets its first price, leave it reduce-only, or paused, until `resume_market`.
- Anything that values a seat's position on a market with accounts other than that market's own: every instruction that values a cross-margin account names every perp market's market, book and price feed, and each is checked for its owner, derivation, kind, market and readiness.
- Anything that opens or ends a price incident, or trips or clears the USDC guard, from an account other than the bound one, or from an observation that is not newer than the last. The one exception is the admin's `set_price_source`, on a paused market with no position and no order, described under "The admin key".
- Anything that lets collateral be withdrawn, moved to a spot balance or paid out of the fee seat while a perp market's source is dislocated or settling. Collateral still moves between seats inside the exchange meanwhile, by liquidation at the settled mark, `cover_shortfall` and moving fees to insurance, as `RULES.md` section 9 lists.
- Anything that returns a market to active status other than `resume_market`, which refuses while any market has a recorded shortfall or the market's own price source is not healthy.
- Anything that lets someone other than the upgrade authority set up the exchange, or someone other than the admin change its settings, add markets, pause, or move the exchange.
- Anything that lets the access lists stop a cancel, a reduce-only order, a transfer, a deposit, a withdrawal, a close, or a target's liquidation, or lets a seat that may not make be filled as a maker, or lets anyone but the admin change a list, a block or a switch.

## What a leaked order key can do

A trader's view holds four order keys. Each trading instruction is signed by one of them and names the key that replaces it. "Used once" is a statement about linkability: a client that never names a key twice gives no two instructions the same signer, so an observer cannot link them. The program refuses a replacement that is one of the four live keys or the owner, but keeps no history of earlier keys and does not look at other signers, so never reusing a key and signing with nothing else are the client's to keep (`DESIGN.md` section 4). It is not a limit on a thief. Whoever holds one live order key names the next key, so a leaked key stays usable, one instruction after another, until the owner replaces all four keys with `set_order_keys`. Nothing expires it and nothing else revokes it.

With one leaked order key an attacker can, for as long as that lasts:

- Place orders for the trader, one per instruction and as many instructions as they like, each at any price inside the market's band around the mark, for as much as the seat's balance or margin allows. On a market where the attacker also has a seat, that is a way to trade with the victim at a price up to the band away from the mark, again and again, which is a transfer of value from the victim to the attacker.
- Cancel the trader's orders, one by one or all of them on a market.
- Liquidate other traders using the victim's seat as the liquidator, within the victim's margin.
- Move the trader's funds between their own spot balance and their own perpetuals collateral, within the withdrawal checks. Nothing leaves the seat or custody, but collateral moved to spot no longer backs a position, and spot funds moved to collateral can be put at risk by an order.
- Name the replacement key each time. The attacker names a key they hold, so the leaked key is a leaked slot that stays theirs.

What an attacker with an order key cannot do:

- Withdraw. `withdraw` is signed by the owner, and the view's address is derived from the owner that signs, so no order key can reach it.
- Close the seat, or replace the four keys. Both are owner-only the same way.
- Read the view, the ledger or a book. Order keys are not members of any permission. The attacker learns the outcome of their order only by its public effects, such as a fill on the tape.
- Use the same key twice. That limits nothing: the key they named in its place is theirs too.

The way back is `set_order_keys`, signed by the owner: it replaces all four keys at once and the attacker's slot is gone. The exposure is bounded by the band, by the seat's balance and margin, and by how long the owner takes to notice, and by nothing else. An order key is not a wallet key: keep it on the trading device, derive the next one from the seed the client documents, and never reuse one.

## What the expiry bounds

Every order-key instruction carries `expires_at`. The program refuses it once the rollup's clock is past that time, or when that time is more than 60 seconds ahead of the rollup's clock. That bounds when an instruction may execute. It says nothing about when it was signed: a signed instruction is valid for whatever window it names, and anyone holding the signed bytes can submit them inside that window. The operator, who orders transactions, can hold one until the last second of it.

## What each privileged key can do

**The gate key** co-signs `open_trader`, and nothing else. Whoever lacks its signature opens no seat at all. Stolen, together with owner keys the thief makes up, it opens seats: each costs the exchange the rent of a view and its permission and takes one of the 2,048 places in the table. The exchange opens no more seats in one UTC day of the rollup's clock than its `max_seats_per_day` setting, and the counted day only moves forward, so a clock that steps back to an earlier day does not start a new count. The loss per day is bounded by that number. The admin closes seats that were never used with `close_unused_trader`, which returns their rent and their place, but only while nobody has touched them: a seat that ever took a deposit, an order or a transfer is no longer the admin's to close, because letting the admin close a funded seat would let the admin take a trader's funds, and a deposit of one atom from anyone is enough. A thief holds the owner keys of the seats it opens, so it can put one atom into each, and those places are then lost for good, since nothing evicts a seat (`RULES.md` section 12) and only the owner can empty and close one. This is accepted. The loss is bounded by `max_seats_per_day` times the days until the admin replaces the gate key or sets the cap to 0 with `update_exchange`, and the places already taken stay taken; at the default cap of 100, the 2,046 trader places are all gone after 21 days, and from then on no one else can open a seat. Closing comes before blocking: any `set_seat_access` change to a seat, a block included, bumps its version, after which `close_unused_trader` refuses it (`SeatUsed`), so the admin closes the stolen gate's unused seats first and blocks only what it cannot close. A cap set to 0 stays 0: once the market has opened, the mainnet profile's setup reports a daily cap that differs from the launch's and leaves it as it is, unless the run is given `RESTORE=seatsPerDay`. The counted openings are public in the exchange account, which is what to watch. A gate that co-signs `open_trader` on request, as the devnet simulation service does with its test grant, gives whoever can ask the same power, so the gate's own policy, not the program, decides who gets the day's places. The gate key cannot move tokens, trade, read anything, or change a setting. The admin replaces it with `update_exchange`.

**The price source** is no key. On mainnet each market reads one Pyth account, fixed when the market is created and derived by the program from the feed id, and the exchange's USDC guard reads USDC's (`RULES.md` sections 9 and 9a). Anyone may hand the program that account with `sync_price` or `sync_quote`, and the program checks the account's address, owner, size, format, verification, feed, exponent, price, confidence and age itself, so a caller chooses only when an observation is read, never what it says. A market's first mark, which no earlier mark bounds, leaves it reduce-only, or paused, until the admin's `resume_market`, so a wrong first observation, or decimals bound wrongly, opens no market before the admin has seen the mark it gives. What the program trusts and cannot check:

- Pyth: its publishers, and the Wormhole guardians whose signatures make an update fully verified.
- The rollup: that its copy of a Pyth account carries the owner and bytes the account has on Solana, which MagicBlock's cloning provides, and that its clock is honest.
- The cranks: an operator keeper, an independent one or anyone else can send a newer observation, but none has to. A caller, a keeper or the operator who orders the rollup's transactions can hold a newer observation back for up to `max_age`, and a fresh mark lags the market by up to Pyth's 0.5% push threshold plus that delay. A feed nobody syncs goes stale, which stops increases, withdrawals by position holders and, after `max_age_liquidation`, liquidation. There is no fallback a key could sign.
- Pyth's confidence in a crash. An observation refused for its confidence (wider than `max_conf_bps`) or its range writes nothing, so it opens no incident however far it is from the mark: the market stays healthy at the old mark, and position holders can still withdraw at that mark until it is `max_age` old, whatever the number of keepers. Confidence commonly widens in a fast crash, so this window tends to open when moves are largest. It is the cost of the confidence rule, which `RULES.md` section 12 notes for `max_conf_bps`, and it is the same window, and the same loss bound, as an observation held back: the gap is realised when a confident print settles it.

**The oracle key** exists on the test networks only. The program built with its `test-oracle` feature lets it write the mark of a market bound to no source, each new price within `max_move_bps` of the last and accepted no sooner than `min_publish_gap` seconds of rollup time after it, whatever publish time it carries. It cannot write a feed's first price, which nothing could bound: a feed that has never had a price refuses `publish_price`, its first is the admin's `reset_price`, and that leaves the market reduce-only until the admin resumes it. So the move limit bounds every price the oracle key writes, and a scale or decimal slip in what it publishes can walk an open market's mark one step at a time but cannot open a market on a wrong price. Stolen, it walks the mark step by step in any direction, one step per gap at most, so prices stamped in the past cannot be stacked into one transaction. That makes healthy positions liquidatable and unhealthy ones safe, moves the band orders are accepted in, moves funding, and lets its holder profit as a trader or a liquidator at the expense of others, up to everything the seats hold as collateral. By not publishing it stops orders that increase exposure and withdrawals by position holders, and, once the feed is older than `max_age_liquidation`, liquidations on that market and by any liquidator holding a position on it. It cannot pay tokens out of custody, change a setting, or read anything private. The admin replaces it with `update_exchange`; `reset_price` then puts the market in reduce-only status, or leaves it paused if it was. The default build, the one mainnet deploys, has neither `publish_price` nor `reset_price`, so there the key moves nothing (`make check-mainnet` checks the binary and its interface file).

**The admin key** changes the gate, the oracle key, the step limit and the daily seat cap; registers tokens and sets each token's custody cap, which can stop deposits but never a withdrawal; creates markets, binding each to its Pyth feed, and changes their limits within the bounds of `RULES.md` section 12 while their source is healthy; pauses the exchange; pauses a market or makes it reduce-only; rebinds a market to another feed with `set_price_source`, but only while it is paused with no position and no order; sets the USDC guard's source and limits, which can stop every perp position from growing but never move money; returns a market to active status with `resume_market`, which is also how a market opens after its first mark; collects everything in the fee seat, except its collateral while a market has a recorded shortfall or a perp's source is not healthy; moves perp fees to the insurance seat and funds it; closes seats that were never used; schedules funding; moves the exchange between Solana and the rollup, and takes the exchange's own lamports on Solana down to its rent. A rebind writes a market's price state, but chooses no price: it clears the mark, the source record and the incident to zero, sets the market's health to healthy and clears the funding totals (`RULES.md` sections 7 and 9). The market is paused with no open interest and no resting order, so nothing is valued at the empty mark, and its next `sync_price` sets the first mark, which leaves it paused until `resume_market`. What the rebind does change: an incident it clears ends at once, settlement window included, so once no perp is unhealthy, collateral withdrawals and the fee seat's collateral payout, the admin's own included, open again without a newer observation. Apart from that rebind it cannot write a price, a source record, an incident or a market's health. It cannot pay a trader's balance out of custody, cannot change which custody account a balance is paid from, cannot return a market to active while any market has a recorded shortfall or the market's own source is unhealthy, and cannot read the ledger, a book or a view through the private endpoint. What it keeps: a pause while a market is settling halts liquidation there and can let a deficit grow, which the program does not prevent. On a test network's build it also resets a price to any value up to `MAX_PRICE` (`RULES.md` section 12), stamped no later than the rollup clock, which has every effect a stolen oracle key has without the move limit and is the only way an unbound market gets its first price, and through that and its own seat it can take traders' collateral by liquidation. The role moves in two steps and the old admin keeps nothing. Every admin action takes effect in the transaction that carries it: the program has no time lock and no delay of its own.

It also sets each perp market's maker and taker list switches, which are public, and lists or blocks seats with `set_seat_access`, which is sealed (`RULES.md` section 3a). So it can stop any seat from making, taking, liquidating or growing a position, at once and with no notice, and clearing a seat's maker bit cancels that seat's resting orders on that market. A block, or a maker list switched on over a live book, stops the seat's old quotes from filling but does not remove them: they stay on the book until matching reaches them or an order that would rest on their full side evicts them, and fill again at their old prices if the seat is unblocked, the list is switched off or the seat is listed first (`RULES.md` section 3a). It can give its own seat a monopoly on making a market, and keep rival liquidators out by blocking them. It can censor; it cannot move funds and cannot block an exit. The lists never refuse a cancel, a reduce-only order, a transfer between balances, a deposit, a withdrawal or a close; the pause, price-incident, margin and shortfall rules still apply to those exactly as they do without the lists. It cannot read the lists back either: they sit in the sealed ledger, so the operator keeps its own signed record of what it set, and a listed party confirms its own flags in its view after a sync.

**The program's upgrade authority** can replace the program, and a replaced program can do anything with every account this one owns, custody included. It is part of the custody trust model whatever the rest of this page says. It is a different thing from the exchange's admin: the upgrade authority sets up the exchange and becomes its first admin, and after that changing one does not change the other. Handing the admin role to a new key leaves the upgrade authority where it was, and moving the upgrade authority leaves the admin where it was. An upgrade takes effect in the slot it lands, with no notice to traders; nothing in this program delays it. How both keys must be held is under "Running a small mainnet market".

## What an observer can learn

The ledger, the books and the views are private through the private endpoint. A custody balance is private when its token was registered sealed: `register_token` then refuses a custody balance that has no private permission, or one that a member can read through. These are not private, and each is a signal:

- **A token registered with public custody.** The admin chooses sealed or public when a token is registered, the exchange records the choice and it never changes. Public custody shows anyone that token's total in custody, and so the size and time of every deposit and withdrawal as the total changes. A depositor's or withdrawer's own token balance is shown too when that account is public, which a deposit or withdrawal through a private endpoint requires, and that ties the amount to that wallet. It does not show resting orders, book depth, seat balances or positions. The endpoint serves no transaction contents, so it does not show which seat was credited or debited either, and anyone can deposit for anyone. Devnet uses public custody because MagicBlock's hosted endpoint refuses a transaction of any program other than the token programs that names a private token balance; the evidence is the table in `spike/devnet/README.md`.

- **Anyone can deposit for anyone.** A deposit names its beneficiary by the address of the beneficiary's view, which anyone derives from the owner's key, so a third party can deposit into any open seat without knowing its number. A seat that holds anything cannot be closed, so an unwanted deposit blocks `close_trader` until the owner withdraws it, and it ends the admin's right to close the seat as unused.
- **A deposit reads the seat last.** Since anyone can deposit for anyone, a refusal that depended on the seat would let a stranger probe any seat's balance, by depositing growing amounts into it, or simulating that, until one is refused. So `deposit` moves the tokens first, and a depositor who does not hold the amount is refused by the token program, whatever the seat holds; it then checks the token's custody against the cap; and only then does it read and credit the seat. The engine does keep a limit per seat, `BALANCE_CAP` = 2^62 atoms of any one balance, so that no credit overflows (`RULES.md` section 12). Asked before the tokens moved, that limit would say which side of `2^62 - x` a seat's balance lies on, and about 63 refused deposits from a stranger holding one atom would read any seat's exact collateral, debt or spot holding, resting orders' locks included. Asked last, after the custody cap, and with `set_custody_cap` refusing a cap above `BALANCE_CAP`, a deposit that passes the cap reaches the seat's limit only for a seat that already holds more than its token's whole custody account, which needs other seats to owe that much there, and only for a depositor who holds the amount and could deposit it. Whether a wallet has a seat at all still shows (below).
- **The custody cap is per token, never per seat.** A deposit is refused when the token's custody account would hold more than the admin's cap afterwards (`RULES.md` section 11). The token's total is what a refusal speaks about, and the program reads it after the tokens moved, so only a depositor who holds the amount gets an answer, and the answer is whether the total is above the cap less that amount. With public custody the total is public anyway. With sealed custody the cap leaks the total to anyone who holds as much as the headroom, `cap - total`. A refusal says whether the total is above `cap - x` for an amount `x` the depositor holds, and a refused deposit costs only the transaction fee, so a binary search over `x` finds the exact total in about log2 of the headroom attempts, and repeating it over time shows how the total moved between probes, so the size and time of deposits and withdrawals to the resolution of the probing, much of what public custody shows. A small cap leaves a small headroom and makes that cheap. Sealed custody keeps its total private only while the cap is far above it, further than anyone could hold, which is a cap that limits little. This matters less in practice than it reads, since a hosted endpoint needs public custody (below). Per-seat exposure is bounded instead by the position cap and the margin rules.
- **A position cap refusal.** An order that could take a seat's position past `position_cap` is refused with a public error, `PositionCapExceeded` (`RULES.md` section 3, check 6). It depends only on the market's settings and the sender's own seat, its position and its open orders on the order's side, which the sender knows already; never on the book. A resting order that the cap would stop, after the admin lowered it or its owner took over a position in a liquidation, fails its owner's fill check when matching reaches it (section 6), an outcome seen by its owner only as an order gone.
- **Whether a known wallet has a seat.** Anyone who knows a wallet can tell whether it has a seat by attempting a deposit into it: a deposit into a wallet with no seat is refused (`AccountMissing`), and one into an open seat is accepted. The wallet's balances, orders and positions stay private.
- **Which order-key instruction a transaction was** does not show in the signature lists of the public accounts: every instruction an order key signs names the same accounts with the same flags (`docs/DESIGN.md` section 1). What those accounts hold can still tell: an order moves the public order counter, a fill prints on the tape.
- **`cover_shortfall`** is callable by anyone for any seat and changes nothing when the seat does not qualify, so the call itself reveals nothing. When it does pay, the market's recorded shortfall, which is public, falls by the amount, and that tells an observer that the named seat was flat with negative collateral and how much it was covered for. A market going reduce-only with a recorded shortfall is public the same way.
- **Open interest** per market is public. It moves with fills, and it moves on a liquidation whenever the liquidator's own position offsets what it takes over. A liquidation adds nothing to the public volume and fill counters and prints nothing on the tape, but a change of open interest with no fill on the tape tells an observer that a liquidation happened on that market and for how many lots net. Read against the tape, open interest also says whether trades opened or closed positions. It moves once per order, by the net of that order's fills. An order with one fill of `s` lots and open interest falling by `s` shows that both counterparties were closing, the buyer a short and the seller a long of at least `s` lots each, and a rise of `s` shows that both were opening; with `taker_side` on the tape, the observer knows which of them was the resting order. This is accepted, as on any venue that publishes open interest. The bound: the counterparties stay anonymous, since the receipts are one-time, an order with several fills shows only their net, and what is learned is a direction and a lower bound on a position, never who holds it or its full size.
- **Liquidation attempts.** A liquidator learns from a successful liquidation that the target was below maintenance margin, and which way it was positioned. The three outcomes with nothing to liquidate (no such seat, no position, not below maintenance) and a liquidation price beyond the liquidator's worst price are recorded in the liquidator's view as one value with every number zero, so an attempt cannot be used to find out whether a seat exists or holds a position. Two outcomes stay distinct. "Stale price" is decided before the target is looked at, from the feed of the market being liquidated and the feeds of every market where the liquidator holds a position, so it says nothing about the target. "Liquidator margin insufficient" only happens when the target was liquidatable, so it does reveal that; this is accepted, since the same liquidator with more margin would have learned it by liquidating. The compute a transaction used is not equalised between those outcomes; whether the private endpoint shows it to the liquidator has not been measured.
- **A full side.** An order whose remainder would rest on a full side evicts a dead order of that side if there is one (expired, or its owner blocked or not on the market's maker list), else the side's worst resting order when its price is strictly better, and is otherwise cancelled as "book full" (`RULES.md` section 3, check 8). Both are written only to the sender's view, but the sender learns which, so a "book full" tells it that the side is full, holds no dead order, and that its worst order is priced at least as well as its own; an order no better than the worst that rests anyway tells it the side held a dead order, expired or barred, not which kind, whose or where. Probing that bound is not free: a probe that rests evicts an order and must then be cancelled. The evicted order's owner learns only that the order is gone, from its own view, as with an order matching cancels; nothing public records an eviction.
- **Post-only probes.** A post-only order that would match is refused whole, and only its sender reads that it was. So any seat can binary-search the best ask with post-only bids, or the best bid with post-only asks, and learn it to the tick: about log2(2 x `band_bps` x mark / (10,000 x tick)) orders, 7 at the deployment's settings. A refused probe fills nothing, pays nothing and prints nothing; a probe that rests must then be cancelled, and can be filled before it is. This is accepted: a book that offers post-only with a private result has it, and without post-only the same probe costs one taker fee and one tape entry. The bound: one price per side, and only inside the crossing band, since an order beyond the band is refused as an error, so a best price further away reads only as "further than the band". Not its size, the depth behind it or its owner. The public order counter and the signature list show that a seat is sending orders, not what it learns (`DESIGN.md` section 1).
- **Access lists.** Each perp market's maker and taker list switches are public; who is listed is not. An access error (`SeatBlocked`, `MakerNotListed`, `TakerNotListed`) tells the public only that some seat sent an order its lists refused, the same class of fact as any other own-state error, and it never depends on the book: an unlisted seat on a market whose maker list is on cannot send a post-only order, so it cannot run the post-only probe above there. The admin's `set_seat_access` names the admin, the exchange, the ledger and the book of every perp market, the same accounts for every seat and every setting, and carries the owner and the flags it sets in its data, in the clear. Who can read that data: the admin, who wrote it; the rollup's operator, which receives and executes every transaction and so sees every transaction's data, as it sees every order's; and anyone the operator or the admin's own machine passes the bytes to. The private endpoint shows the transaction's accounts, logs and data to nobody, the admin included (`tests/audit/access-lists.ts`). So the lists are sealed against other traders and the public, not against the operator, which is the same trust the operator already carries for ordering and for what it commits (below). Of those accounts only the admin's key and the exchange are public, as for `move_fees_to_insurance`. The operator's tools send the call behind a compute budget instruction, on every call, since the admin cannot read a seat's flags to know whether one clears a maker bit. The Compute Budget program is not a sealed account, so its signature list, which the private endpoint serves to anyone, names every transaction that carries the instruction. No trader's call carries one, and the only other budgeted transactions are the admin's growth of the exchange's accounts at setup, so in steady state that list tells anyone that the admin sent an access change at that time, and when (`tests/audit/access-lists.ts` proves the list names the call). That adds the kind of call to what the paragraph already concedes ("the admin did something at that time"); it does not name the owner or the market, and through the same endpoint a stranger is shown none of the call's accounts, logs or data, nor the compute it used (the same test). It shows that the admin did something at that time, not for whom or on which market, though a new maker quoting right after can be correlated with it; batch changes or list ahead of time. The exchange's signature list also shows each call's status and error code, as for every admin instruction. A `set_seat_access` that failed with `SeatNotOpen` (6011), which no other admin instruction returns once the exchange is set up, tells an observer that the admin named an owner with no open seat, though not which owner; a failure for lack of compute (below) shows the same way. `ops/access.ts` therefore simulates each call first, through the same endpoint, and sends nothing when the simulation fails or the endpoint does not simulate; a simulation lands in no signature list. Any other tool the admin uses must do the same. With one listed maker, every fill's maker is that seat, so its inventory on that market can be rebuilt from the tape and the public open interest; the same holds for a lone listed taker. Maker privacy holds only once several makers are listed, and a market maker should be told so before it is listed.

## What this program does not guarantee

- Confidentiality of reads is the rollup's query filter. The program attaches the permissions; the rollup enforces them. The rollup's own port serves everything to anyone who can reach it, and on a hosted validator that port is the operator's promise.
- A custody permission against a change of the programs that guard it. The program checks the permission when the token is registered and not again. That is enough as those programs stand, because every way to change it needs the signature of the token's custody authority, an address of this program that signs one thing only, an SPL Token transfer out of that token's custody:
  - The token program's `ResetEphemeralAtaPermission` and `UndelegateEphemeralAtaPermission` both require a signer equal to the balance's owner (`IncorrectAuthority` and `InvalidAccountData` otherwise). Creating the permission is open to anyone, but only with no read flags, and it does nothing when the permission exists.
  - The permission program's own update takes the signature of a member with the authority flag or of the guarded balance. The only member the token program ever writes is the balance's owner, and the balance is an address of the token program, which signs for it only in the instructions above.
  - This program has no instruction that signs as the custody authority towards either program, so nobody can loosen a custody permission, the admin included.

  This was read from the interface in `ephemeral-rollups-sdk` 0.17.3 and the token program's published source, not from the deployed bytes. Both programs are upgradeable by their own authority, and an upgrade could change the rule; the program would not notice.

- A private endpoint takes no transaction of this program that names a private token balance. The local filter (query-filtering-service 0.1.3) refuses it whoever sends it and whatever flags its permission gives the sender; a plain SPL transfer that names the same balance passes. The hosted devnet endpoint (magicblock-core 1.0.0, measured 2026-10-09) does the same, for the balance's own owner too, and also when the permission exists on Solana without being delegated; it accepts the SPL Token and Associated Token programs, and this program when every token balance it names is public. With sealed custody, `register_token`, `deposit`, `withdraw`, `collect_fees` and `fund_insurance` therefore go to the rollup's own port (`DEPOSIT_URL` in the Makefile, `depositUrl` in the deployment description), which only the local network has. Through a hosted endpoint they need a public custody balance and a public depositor or recipient balance, which is why devnet's tokens are registered public. That is the endpoint's rule, not a property of the program.
- A public custody against a permission attached later. `register_token` checks once that a public custody balance has no permission. The token program lets any payer create a permission for any balance: simulated on Solana devnet on 2026-10-10, a stranger's `createEataPermission` for a delegated public custody balance of an unsignable owner succeeded, at the stranger's cost of the permission's rent. A permission that exists, delegated or not, makes the hosted endpoint refuse this program's transactions that name the balance. So a stranger can probably stop deposits, withdrawals and fee collection of a public-custody token through the hosted endpoint, and the recorded visibility would then no longer describe the balance. It was not carried out against a registered custody. Tokens could not be taken this way; they could be stuck until the endpoint or the token program changes.
- A custody balance against a stranger who claims it before registration. The token program lets any payer create any owner's balance and delegate it to any validator, and only the owner can undelegate it. A stranger who delegates a custody balance to a validator nobody runs, or attaches a permission to it, or delegates its permission elsewhere, makes it useless to this exchange for good: the balance never reaches this rollup, or registration refuses it. No tokens are at risk, since registration or the first deposit fails before any moves. Each token's custody authority is derived from a nonce the admin chooses when the token is registered, and the exchange records that nonce with the token, so a custody address is not known before the admin uses it and a claimed address is skipped by choosing another nonce. The admin picks the nonce at random and keeps it private until the custody balance is created and delegated, and the set-up checks that the balance's delegation record names this exchange's validator and that a public custody has no permission, failing loudly otherwise. That closes the claim before the balance exists, and it closes a sealed custody fully, since its balance and permission are created and delegated in one transaction. A public custody balance is still public from its delegation until `register_token` lands in the rollup, seconds later or up to a minute, and a permission a stranger attaches in that window makes registration refuse it as `CustodyNotPublic`, with no tokens at risk. The admin then registers under another nonce, and each attempt can be raced the same way. This is the same limitation as a public custody against a permission attached later, above, moved earlier; the nonce does not prevent it. Once a token is registered its nonce never changes, so every payout signs with the custody it was registered with.
- Ordering. The rollup operator can see, order, delay or drop transactions inside the rollup. An order can execute any time before its expiry.
- What the rollup's validator commits. Every account this program keeps inside the rollup, the custody balances included, is delegated to the exchange's validator, which executes the transactions and commits the resulting state of those accounts to Solana. The program checks every instruction it is given; it cannot check that the validator ran it, or that what the validator commits is what the instructions produced. A validator that commits state of its own making can credit any seat and move any custody balance, so it is trusted for the integrity of every delegated account, as it is trusted with ordering. The set-up only checks that each custody balance is delegated to that validator (`tests/audit/custody-foreign-validator.ts`). A rollback, the rollup resuming from an earlier state, undoes every fill, liquidation, deposit and withdrawal after that state together, so inside the rollup custody still equals the seats; but what had already left the rollup in between stays where it went. A token balance withdrawn and then moved back to Solana before the rollback is out, while the rewound ledger credits the seat again, so the same tokens can be paid twice, and every trader may act again on a state the others saw replaced. Withdrawal back to Solana is not part of this program, and neither is any protection against this.
- A delay between an admin action or an upgrade and its effect. The program has no time lock: a setting change, a price reset, a pause, an admin hand-over or a program upgrade takes effect in the transaction that carries it, and traders learn of it afterwards.
- That one order reaches live liquidity behind dead orders. `RULES.md` section 4 counts each resting order that matching cancels (expired, outside the crossing band, failing its fill check, or the taker's own) as one of the `MAX_STEPS` steps. A trader can rest orders that will have expired by the time a taker arrives, ahead of live ones, and that taker's order then ends at the step limit with nothing filled. This is accepted. Each such attempt removes every dead order it reached, `MAX_STEPS` of them, so the taker's next attempt goes further and one seat's 32 dead orders on a market cost a taker at most two empty attempts, and keeping one taker blocked needs a fresh set of dead orders between each two of its attempts, which the attacker cannot see in the private rollup and whose ordering only the operator controls. A separate, larger budget for removing dead orders would only move the bound: one seat may rest 32 orders on a market, already twice the deployment's 16 steps, any number of seats can outgrow a fixed budget, and each further seat a call may touch costs another journal entry on the program's heap and more compute. Dead orders do not also lock makers out: an order that would rest on a full side evicts a dead one wherever it sits (`RULES.md` section 3, check 8). That is an expired order, or one whose owner may not make on that market as things stand: the seat is blocked, or the maker list is on and does not list it (`RULES.md` section 3a). Such an order could fill again if its owner were unblocked or listed, but one that would rest is live now, so the barred one gives way; an admin who wants a seat's quotes back unchanged unblocks it before the side fills. Two kinds of dead order still hold a slot until matching or their owner removes them, because either can come alive again: one outside the crossing band since the mark moved, and a perp order whose owner would now fail the fill check. Making either takes more than choosing an expiry: the mark has to move, or the owner's own margin has to fall. The position cap is not a way to make them at will: an order is accepted only if it, the open orders on its side and the position on that side fit under the cap together (`RULES.md` section 3, check 6), so every order the cap lets rest can fill in full. A resting order fails the cap only after the admin lowers it, or after its owner takes over a position in a liquidation; like the others it is cancelled the first time matching reaches it, and placing it again is refused.
- The price. On mainnet the mark is Pyth's, as the rollup sees it and as fast as someone syncs it ("The price source" above). It is bounded by the move limit and the ages, and a jump beyond the limit is realised by a settlement rather than prevented. On the test networks one oracle key writes it. Either way the program bounds how fast the mark moves, not where it goes: which feed a market is bound to, or what the oracle publishes, is outside the program, and a source that can be pushed is followed to wherever it is pushed. "Running a small mainnet market" says what the source must be.
- That a perp pays exactly SOL priced in USDC. It pays USD points settled one for one in USDC (`RULES.md` section 1), which differ by USDC's distance from a dollar, at most `depeg_bps` for any position that grows by trading: while the USDC guard does not pass, no fill grows a perp position, and a resting order whose fill would is cancelled when matching reaches it (`RULES.md` section 9a). A liquidator's takeover still grows the liquidator's own position, by its choice, so liquidation keeps working through a depeg. Solvency does not depend on it: every balance is in USDC atoms.
- That no position goes bankrupt before it is liquidated, or that a bankrupt one finds a liquidator. Liquidation is open to anyone, but liquidators cannot see who is unhealthy, and taking over a bankrupt position pays nothing beyond a fill at the mark (`RULES.md` section 8), with no backstop of the venue's own to take it (section 12b). So someone has to liquidate before bankruptcy, and until someone takes a bankrupt position its deficit moves with the mark. When a target ends flat with negative equity, insurance pays that deficit, and whoever held the other side of the losing trades keeps the matching gain. If that other side is a second seat of the same owner, the owner is up by the deficit, paid out of insurance. This is bad debt from a move past bankruptcy, which every perpetuals venue carries, and it is bounded as follows:
  - How far the mark moves before a liquidation lands. The mark moves at most `max_move_bps` of the current mark per `min_publish_gap` seconds of rollup time (section 9), and section 12 sizes that move, with one interval of funding at the cap, so that one price update leaves a seat that was at maintenance with at least the penalty at the moved mark, long or short. So at least two updates take a seat from maintenance to zero equity. How many more depends on the side, because the limit applies to the current mark and so compounds: a long loses `1 - (1 - move)^n` of its starting notional in `n` updates, a short `(1 + move)^n - 1`. At the deployed settings (`ops/deployment.ts`: maintenance 5%, penalty 2%, move 2.5%, gap 1 s, funding at most 0.5% per 8 h) a long that was above maintenance needs three updates to reach zero equity, since two move the mark down at most 1 - 0.975^2 = 4.94%, short of its 5% margin, but a short can reach it in two, since two move the mark up as much as 1.025^2 - 1 = 5.06%. So insurance pays only when no liquidation lands between the update that makes the seat liquidatable and the one that makes it bankrupt: at least two seconds for a long, but only one `min_publish_gap`, one second, for a short. From then on the deficit grows by at most 2.5% of the position's notional at the current mark per update, and updates come at most once a second. This is the per-gap bound, and it holds between settlements.
  - How far a settled gap moves the mark. When the source moves beyond `max_move_bps`, the market is dislocated and nothing on it trades, liquidates or pays out; a second, newer observation settles it, and the mark jumps to the source in one step (`RULES.md` section 9). The per-gap bound does not hold across that step. What a settlement can cost beyond margin is at most `open_interest_cap * mark * (gap - liq_penalty_bps)`, with `mark` the mark the gap starts from and the gap in bps of it, since a position waiting to be liquidated still holds at least the penalty (above): a 20% gap at a 2% penalty leaves at most 18% of the notional at that mark uncovered on the losing side. Insurance and then the fee seat's collateral pay it as far as they reach, and the rest is recorded as a shortfall that stops withdrawals. A test network's admin `reset_price` likewise has no move limit and can put a position past bankruptcy in one step.
  - The size of a position. A position is at most the market's `open_interest_cap` of total long size, 10,000,000 lots at the deployed settings: about 1,500,000 nUSD of notional on `NSOL-PERP` and 18,000,000 nUSD on `NNVDA-PERP` at their initial marks. An order may take one seat to at most `position_cap` lots, counting the position and the open orders on its side, 2,000,000 at the deployed settings, though a liquidator may take over past it. Those caps are test-network figures, not a bound a small fund can absorb; `RULES.md` section 12 gives the formula a real market's cap is sized by.
  - What insurance and the fee seat hold. Insurance pays first, then the fee seat's collateral, and only beyond both is the deficit recorded on the market, which goes reduce-only and stops every collateral withdrawal (section 8, step 5). So collected perp fees absorb a small deficit before it freezes anyone, unless the admin has already collected them.

  One more case falls under the same bound. The penalty cap is computed at the prices the liquidation uses, with the target's other markets at their last price, fresh or stale (section 8). A target can pay a full penalty on one market, keep a position on another whose price later moves against it, such as a stale feed catching up, and end bankrupt there; insurance then covers a deficit that the earlier penalty made larger. The first bound is only as good as the liquidations that land, which is why "Running a small mainnet market" below asks the operator to run its own.

- A custody cap against tokens sent from outside. The cap counts the custody account's whole balance (`RULES.md` section 11), and anyone may send tokens into a custody account with a plain token transfer. Doing so uses up headroom and can stop deposits until the admin raises the cap. It costs the sender those tokens for good, since no instruction pays them out, and it moves no seat's balance. The admin's `fund_insurance` is not held to the cap either, so funding insurance near the cap leaves less room for traders.
- Anything outside the rollup. Withdrawal back to Solana is not part of this program.

## Running a small mainnet market

Nothing in this repository has run on mainnet, and `RULES.md` section 12b lists what is not built before real money. The limits this page accepts are acceptable for a small market only under these operating conditions, which the program does not enforce. The mainnet profile of `ops/network.ts` sets up the settings below from `ops/deployment.ts`, whose unit tests hold them to the figures on this page; `make rehearsal-setup` runs it on devnet and `make mainnet-setup` on mainnet (README, "Deploying"). It and the crank, the keeper and the smoke send nothing until the program deployed at their id is, byte for byte, the build `make check-mainnet DECLARED_ID=<id>` made for that id, with no instruction that writes a price, and it refuses a market already on the network whose settings or price source are not the ones below. Every run names the genesis it expects, and on mainnet they also stop unless the rollup they are pointed at serves mainnet's USDC mint, so an endpoint and validator identity copied from a test network, which pass the identity check together, cannot get the exchange delegated to a rollup that does not work from mainnet. Setup is safe to run again before and after launch, but once the market has opened it no longer writes back the three launch settings an admin changes in an incident, the daily seat cap, the USDC guard and the market's status: it says which differ from the launch and leaves them, and writes back only the one a run names with `RESTORE=seatsPerDay`, `quoteSource` or `resume` (the last after the same mark check as at launch). A `RESUME_MARKET=yes` left from the launch opens nothing after it.

- **The gate co-signs `open_trader` only after the operator's own check** of who is asking, with a limit per person, never on request. A gate that signs on request lets anyone use up the seat table for good. The same check is the geofence: the gate declines people in places the operator does not serve, the United States and Ontario among them as on other venues, and the front end shows a risk warning before a first deposit.
- **`max_seats_per_day` is close to the number of seats the operator expects to admit in a day**, well below the default of 100, and the openings counted in the exchange account are watched.
- **The operator runs a liquidation keeper**: a funded seat of its own that tries `liquidate` against every open seat on every perp market, again and again, and takes bankrupt positions at the mark. Liquidation is open to anyone, but it is blind and a bankrupt position pays its liquidator nothing, so nobody else can be counted on to do it in time. A keeper would have to sweep after every price update to close the bound above: at the deployed settings a short can go from liquidatable to bankrupt in one `min_publish_gap`. A keeper that holds a position on a market whose feed is older than `max_age_liquidation` gets "stale price" on every attempt. `ops/keeper.ts` (`make mainnet-keeper`) is such a keeper, and it does not close that bound. Nobody can read the ledger through the private endpoint, the operator included, so it tries every seat blind, four at a time, one per order key, at the mark read before each four, and reads each outcome from its own view; positions it takes over stay on its seat until the operator closes them. A sweep covers every seat below a bound. Nothing public counts the seats opened before the current UTC day, only the exchange's count of that day's openings, so the bound starts from `KEEPER_SEATS`, which the operator sets from the gate's own records to the highest seat index the gate has opened plus one (the fee and insurance seats are indices 0 and 1, so 40 seats opened with none closed is `KEEPER_SEATS=42`). The keeper adds one day's openings as a margin, adds every opening the exchange counts while it runs, keeps the bound in a file with the keys across restarts, takes every day it did not see as a full day of openings, and warns at start that a seat above the bound is never liquidated. A sweep takes about one round trip to the rollup per four seats, and sweeps follow one another `KEEPER_EVERY` seconds apart, 1 by default. So a seat waits up to a whole sweep and a pause between becoming liquidatable and the attempt that takes it, and through that wait a short's deficit grows by up to 2.5% of its notional for every second of price updates, which insurance pays. Keep the table small enough for a sweep to stay within a few seconds, and run more than one keeper over parts of it as it grows. The keeper logs a liquidatable seat its own margin cannot take as an alert, counts a perp market whose market or price feed account it cannot read as a failed sweep, and exits non-zero after `KEEPER_GIVE_UP` failed sweeps in a row. A market where every attempt reads "stale price" is no failed sweep, since a price incident does that for a while, but the keeper can liquidate nobody there, so it also exits non-zero once one market has read stale on every sweep for longer than `KEEPER_STALE_GIVE_UP`, 1,800 seconds by default, twice the deployed settle window. It runs under a supervisor that restarts it and tells someone.
- **Two keepers sync every price.** An operator keeper and an independent one read each market's Pyth account and USDC's every second or two and send `sync_price` or `sync_quote` for each newer observation. A read costs nothing but the read, and a sync is sent only for a newer observation. Pyth pushes on a 0.5% move and its 55 second heartbeat; a market nobody syncs goes stale. The move limit is why the pace matters: an observation more than `max_move_bps` (2.5%) from the mark dislocates the market, and a second one still beyond it settles it (`RULES.md` section 9), so a dislocation means a real gap only while the cranks keep up. Synced every second or two, a continuous move dislocates only when it covers more than 2.5% between two observations the program takes, faster than Pyth's own 0.5% steps arrive in practice; a crank that reads every 10 seconds would turn a 3% move over 8 seconds into one jump, a dislocation and then a settlement. A crank that backs off after errors, up to 60 seconds, falls behind in the same way, which is one reason for the second, independent one. Every crank round has a deadline (`CRANK_DEADLINE`, 60 seconds) and every keeper sweep one (`KEEPER_DEADLINE`, 180 seconds): a request that never answers, as one did for 18 minutes in a devnet outage, ends the round as failed and the loop goes on, counted toward giving up like any failed round. `ops/crank.ts` (`make mainnet-crank`) is the operator's: it reads every Pyth account every `CRANK_EVERY` seconds, 1 by default, and logs how old each observation is when the rollup sees it, which is the soak below, and the market's status and price health or the guard's state after each round. A sync the program takes can be the one that opens an incident, a dislocated or settling market or a depegged guard, so a round that leaves one open counts as a failure like a refused sync, and so does a perp market whose market or price feed account cannot be read, and the crank stops after a run of them, so it runs under a supervisor that restarts it and tells someone. Both the crank and the keeper ride out an outage of an RPC endpoint (a 5xx, a 429, a refused connection, a timeout) by counting each errored round as a failure and pausing longer, up to 60 seconds, and give up only after the configured run of failed rounds (`CRANK_GIVE_UP`, `KEEPER_GIVE_UP`), so a supervisor must restart them on exit and alert on it.
- **Each market is bound to a Pyth feed that aggregates several independent publishers and venues**, never one that follows this exchange's own book or tape, a single venue, or one thin pool. A stale feed stops risk-taking on its own, a wrong one does not. The largest losses on other perpetuals venues came from paying out at a mark the attacker could push: the move limit and the settlement slow such a push, they do not stop it. The admin resumes a market after its first mark only once that mark has been checked against other sources. The mainnet profile resumes the market last, once insurance, the custody cap and the keeper are in place. It prints the mark beside the median of the SOL/USD last-trade prices of three public exchanges (Coinbase, Kraken and Bitstamp, at fixed addresses, with each source and its price), taken from at least two that answered: with an odd number of answers it is the middle price, and with an even number the two middle prices must agree within 1% or the check is refused, and resumes only on a run given `RESUME_MARKET=yes`, with the mark and that median within 1%. When `PYTH_PRO_TOKEN` is set, Pyth Pro (`pyth.dourolabs.app`) is one more source in the same median. The exchanges are independent of Pyth, so the check catches a decimal slip or a wrongly bound feed; it trusts that the public endpoints are not all wrong at once.
- **The liquidation keeper sweeps every seat when a market starts settling**, since the settled mark is when the gap is realised.
- **Caps are small and insurance is funded first.** The admin funds insurance before trading opens and sizes `open_interest_cap` from it with the formula in `RULES.md` section 12: `open_interest_cap <= insurance / (mark * (g - penalty))` for the largest gap `g` planned for, at least the 20% a settled gap is sized for, with `mark` the mark the gap would start from, so a gap settled across the whole cap is covered. With 3,000 USDC of insurance, a 20% gap and a 2% penalty that is 111,111 lots of 0.001 SOL, about 16,700 USDC of long notional at 150 USDC per SOL, against 10,000,000 lots on the test network. At a higher mark the same insurance covers fewer lots, 66,666 at 250 USDC per SOL, so the mainnet profile prints what insurance covers at the live mark and keeps the market reduce-only while that is below the cap, at any mark above 150 USDC. The admin then lowers `open_interest_cap` with `update_market` to at most what the refusal names, and `position_cap` with it, and runs setup again: the profile takes a market whose caps are at most the launch's, with `position_cap` at most `open_interest_cap`, and compares every other setting exactly. What insurance covers is computed from the 3,000 USDC the launch funds, not from what the insurance seat holds, which is sealed and which nothing public shows; so the tooling does not see insurance that a deficit drained, nor insurance funded later by hand. The operator keeps that figure, and checks the cap against it again whenever insurance falls or the mark moves far from the price it was sized at. `position_cap` is at most a fifth of it, so no one seat holds a market-sized position, and a position at the cap is small against what the source venues trade: pushing the source against it must cost more than the position can gain. Every perp fee goes to insurance (`fee_insurance_share_bps` = 10,000) until insurance reaches a stated multiple of the loss it is sized for.
- **Custody caps are small and raised in steps.** Each token's `custody_cap` starts at zero when it is registered, so nothing is deposited before the admin chooses a cap. It is set to what the team could make good while withdrawal back to Solana is unproven, and raised only after each stage of admission has run for a stated time with no invariant failing. Nothing in `ops/` raises a cap on mainnet by itself: the smoke, which raises one on a rehearsal when its deposit meets it, stops there instead, and runs on mainnet only with its own `MAINNET_SMOKE=yes`, since it deposits the admin's USDC into two seats and trades them on the public tape. On a live market its orders can fill against other traders, so on mainnet it only tops each seat up to its 10 USDC, and however it ends it cancels both seats' orders and closes both positions with reduce-only orders, logging what it could not close; the USDC stays in the two seats, whose keys are kept like the admin's. Insurance and the funding schedule are each sent once per program: recorded with the keys under an id of their own before they are sent, so a run that stops before it learns the outcome stops every later run until the operator answers for that record by its id, `landed:<id>` or `lost:<id>`. A `lost` sends once more under a new id, so the same answer, left in a shell or recalled from its history, never sends twice. Only one setup run at a time holds the keys' lock, and a run whose gate key is not the exchange's stops before any step, which catches a run from a copy of the keys that lacks these records. What the program can show is checked too: with public custody, `fund_insurance` is refused while custody already holds the insurance amount, which before launch only an earlier `fund_insurance` explains, unless the operator answers with the balance it names. Nothing public shows whether funding was scheduled, and with sealed custody nothing shows insurance, so there the records with the keys are the only guard: losing them, or answering `lost` for a send that landed, sends again, and insurance sent twice stays in the insurance seat for good, since nothing pays insurance out.
- **Launch settings are plain.** One market, the SOL perpetual (and SOL spot, if any), and no other asset until the market has run. At most 5x: `im_bps` 2,000, with `mm_bps` 500, `liq_penalty_bps` 200, `band_bps` 400 and `max_move_bps` 250, which pass every rule of `RULES.md` section 12. A perp taker fee of 5 bps and a spot taker fee of 5 to 10 bps, and none for makers on either, with the funding cap kept at 50 bps per 8 hours; the perp fee must stay at least 1 bp while funding is on (section 7). A lot of 0.001 SOL (`base_lot` 1,000,000 at 9 decimals) and a tick of 10 quote atoms per lot, 0.01 USDC per SOL or about 0.7 bps at 150, finer than the test network's 100. A finer tick spreads thin liquidity over more price levels, which is acceptable with one market and one operator maker, and makes the post-only probe of "What an observer can learn" dearer: about 11 orders instead of 7.
- **The ages are measured before deposits.** A read-only `sync_price` simulation against the hosted rollup, and a soak of several hours comparing the rollup's clock with each observation's publish time and the delay before the rollup sees it, set `max_age` and `max_age_liquidation`; `ops/deployment.ts` holds 90 and 180 seconds until then.
- **The upgrade authority is a multisig, and the admin key lives on a hardware wallet.** The multisig has signers who keep their keys on separate devices, a threshold no one person meets, and a check of every transaction's decoded instructions before signing it. No signer ever signs a transaction in advance against a durable nonce: such a transaction never expires, and whoever later holds it can send it when they choose, which is how an admin council can be emptied without any key being stolen. A transaction is built, checked and signed when it is to be sent. As built, the exchange lives in the rollup while it runs and the admin signs each of its instructions there, as a key in the transaction; only setting up, delegating and emptying the exchange are signed on Solana. A multisig that signs only through its own program on Solana, such as a Squads multisig, therefore cannot hold the admin role, while the upgrade authority, which signs on Solana, can be one.

  The admin role is therefore held by one key on a hardware wallet (a Ledger, used through a wallet such as Phantom). It signs only through the operator console, which shows each transaction's decoded instructions, network and genesis before the device signs. The key never exists outside the device, and is used for nothing but this exchange. This is weaker than a multisig, and the bound is stated plainly: whoever holds the admin key can pause, change limits within RULES 12, set custody caps, block or list seats and resume markets, but cannot move funds or stop an exit. The upgrade authority, which can replace the program and so take everything, stays on the multisig. If the admin key or its device is ever in doubt, the role moves to a new hardware key at once through `propose_admin` and `accept_admin`, checked out of band. Before `resume_market` on mainnet, the admin must not be the upgrade authority (the launch profile refuses otherwise).

- **The upgrade authority is not the admin**, and once the program is final it is set to none, so the program can no longer be replaced. `initialize` takes the upgrade authority as the first admin, so the mainnet profile sets up with one key in both roles, and it keeps the market reduce-only on mainnet while the admin is still the upgrade authority: the upgrade authority moves to its multisig, or the admin role to its new holder, before the run that resumes it. That check proves only that the two keys differ. It cannot prove that the upgrade authority is a multisig: a multisig signs from an address no key signs for, but so is any program's address, and nothing on chain shows a threshold. So the operator confirms the upgrade authority's multisig and threshold in the multisig's own program before giving the resume run its yes. Its targets name the program they send to, `MAINNET_PROGRAM_ID=<id>`, rather than taking a yes that a shell could keep for every later run. Until then the plan is a time lock outside the program: both roles held by a multisig whose proposals wait a fixed delay, at least a day, before they can run, and are public while they wait, so traders see a pending upgrade or setting change and can withdraw first. The program itself has no time lock (above).
- **The admin role moves only through `propose_admin` and `accept_admin`**, and both parties confirm the nominee's address through a second channel before the acceptance is signed.
- **Custody visibility is chosen per token before it is registered**, and never changes. Sealed custody hides the totals but needs the rollup's own port for deposits and withdrawals, which a hosted endpoint does not offer. Public custody works through the hosted endpoint, and shows each token's total and the size of every deposit and withdrawal, with the open risk of a permission a stranger attaches (above).
- **A market's maker list is switched on over an empty book**, after its maker is listed with `set_seat_access` and before `resume_market` opens a new market. Switching it on over a live book leaves the unlisted orders in place until takers clear them, `MAX_STEPS` at a time, each one a step, or a listed maker's order evicts them one at a time from a full side. If the lone maker stops quoting, nobody can close except against its quotes or by liquidation; switching the maker list off is the instant remedy, and it also lets every unlisted seat's order still on that book fill again at its old price. The liquidation keeper is never blocked: it needs no listing and unwinds with reduce-only orders.
- **A seat's old quotes are removed before it may make again.** A block, and a maker list switched on over a live book, leave the seat's resting orders on the book, and they fill at their old prices once the seat may make there again (`RULES.md` section 3a). Before unblocking a seat or listing it as a maker, the operator tells it to send `cancel_all` on every market, or removes the orders itself: clearing a maker bit cancels the seat's orders on that market, so it unblocks the seat with its maker bits cleared and sets them again in a second call, and for a market where the bit was never set it sets the bit and then clears it before listing the seat for real. A blocked seat's spot orders come back with the unblock too, and since the lists name perp markets only, only the seat's own cancel removes those. The operator cannot see which unlisted seats still have orders on a book, so switching a maker list off accepts that any of them may fill.
- **A `set_seat_access` that clears maker bits carries a compute budget.** Clearing a bit scans that market's book for the seat's orders and closes the gaps they leave, so on full books it can need more than the 200,000 compute units a transaction of one instruction gets by default, and the SDK's `setSeatAccess` adds no budget instruction. The operator sends `ComputeBudgetProgram.setComputeUnitLimit({ units: 1_400_000 })` in front of it, and clears bits on one market per call when the books are full. A call that runs out changes nothing, so no seat is ever stuck; it only has to be sent again with a budget. A block or a setting that clears no maker bit scans no book. `tests/audit/access-lists.ts` measures a delisting against a full side.
- **`position_cap` binds the lone maker first.** It applies to the maker like any seat (`RULES.md` section 3, check 6): its position plus its open orders on a side plus the new order must stay within the cap, so its quotes on the side it is already long or short start failing with `PositionCapExceeded` before any taker's do. While one maker carries a market, raise `position_cap` toward `open_interest_cap` and rely on the gate's per-person limit for retail; the mainnet profile's setup accepts a `position_cap` up to `open_interest_cap`.
- **A block is per seat, not per person.** A blocked owner can close its seat and open a new one, which starts unlisted and unblocked, so the gate's own per-person denylist must carry every block. Any `set_seat_access` change to a seat that was never used, listing and blocking alike, bumps its version, after which `close_unused_trader` refuses it (`SeatUsed`), so seats that may never trade are not listed ahead of time, and an unused seat to be removed is closed before it is blocked.
